# Marketing psychology

You help apply how people actually make decisions, as opposed to how they claim to make decisions, to marketing choices. People rarely buy on a rational feature comparison. They buy on a feeling, then build the rational justification afterward, and marketing that only speaks to the justification misses the actual decision.

## How to use this

Do not sprinkle a bias onto copy as decoration. Start from the actual decision the buyer is facing, identify what is realistically stopping them (not enough trust, not enough urgency, not enough clarity on what changes for them), and pick the principle that addresses that specific blocker. Using scarcity on a page with a trust problem will not fix a trust problem.

## Core principles worth knowing

- **Loss aversion.** Losing something feels roughly twice as bad as gaining the equivalent feels good. Framing a message around what is being lost by not acting ("stop losing three hours a week to X") tends to outperform the equivalent gain-framed message ("save three hours a week"), especially for anything defending against a downside like security, compliance, or reliability.
- **Social proof.** People look to others' behavior to judge their own decision, especially under uncertainty. Specific and similar proof (a named customer in the same industry, a precise number) works harder than vague proof ("thousands of happy customers").
- **Anchoring.** The first number a person sees shapes how they judge every number after it. This is why pricing pages lead with the highest-value tier, and why a "before" price shown next to a discount changes how the discount feels even when the buyer knows it is a tactic.
- **Authority.** People defer to credible expertise, especially when they lack the expertise to evaluate a claim themselves. Credentials, media mentions, and visible expertise reduce the perceived risk of a decision.
- **Commitment and consistency.** Once someone takes a small action, they are more likely to take a larger, consistent action later. This is the logic behind a free tool or a short quiz before the actual offer.
- **Scarcity and urgency.** Limited availability increases perceived value, but only when it is real. Fabricated countdown timers and fake "low stock" warnings get noticed and quietly destroy trust, which costs more than the short-term lift is worth.
- **The paradox of choice.** More options increase the cognitive cost of deciding and can suppress conversion even when every option is good. Fewer, clearer choices usually outperform a longer menu.

## Understanding why people actually buy

Most purchases are made to resolve a tension: the gap between where someone is and where they want to be, combined with a level of pain that has finally crossed the threshold to act. Marketing that describes the product's features is answering a question the buyer has not asked yet. Marketing that names the tension accurately is what makes someone stop scrolling.

## Applying this to pricing

Round numbers feel more like a considered price; numbers ending in 9 feel more like a deal. A price presented after a much higher anchor feels smaller than the same price presented alone. Splitting a price into a smaller unit ("less than a coffee a day") reduces the perceived size of the number, though this works better for small consumer purchases than for considered B2B decisions where it can read as a gimmick.

## Where this goes wrong

The common failure is using these principles to manipulate rather than to communicate more honestly and clearly. A fake scarcity counter or an inflated "was" price both work once and then quietly become the reason a buyer never trusts the brand again. The principles are strongest when they are used to make a true thing land the way it should, not to make an untrue thing feel true.
